Analysing Ideas and Values

Achievement Standard 91394 requires perceptive analysis of the classical world. You must demonstrate profound insight into the didactic purpose of Homer's Odyssey and the complexities of ancient Greek socio-political structures.

The Path to Excellence

Examiners are looking for "perception." This means moving beyond describing what happened in the text to analysing why it was written that way.

  • ✦
    Textual Connections: Seamlessly weaving direct quotes (primary evidence) into your arguments.
  • ✦
    Complexity: Showing how values like Xenia or Dike conflict within the narrative.
  • ✦
    Wider Implications: Drawing conclusions that connect the Odyssey to its socio-political context.

Origins & Authorship

Debates about authorship — the Homeric Question — emerged in the 1800s. Scholarly consensus posits that the epics developed within an oral tradition spanning several centuries before being recorded in writing.

"The epics straddled the beginnings of widespread literacy — their linguistic characteristics can be traced to an earlier era." — Robert Lamberton

The Wayfinder's Bridge

Excellence candidates should consider wider cultural implications: how the poem was made, how it has been read since, and how its values compare with — and differ from — other traditions.

🌊

Xenia & Manaakitanga: A Discussed Analogy

The parallel between xenia and manaakitanga is a genuinely recognised comparison in NZ Classical Studies teaching — but it's an active discussion point among scholars, not a settled one-to-one equivalence, so treat it as a starting point for analysis rather than a fact to quote outright.

  • • Xenia: Protected by Zeus Xenios; failure results in divine wrath (e.g., the Suitors).
  • • Manaakitanga: Grounded in mana and reciprocal care within tikanga Māori; failure diminishes the mana of the host.
  • • Worth noting: some scholars caution against flattening manaakitanga into a simple "Māori word for hospitality," since it carries relational and spiritual dimensions that don't map neatly onto a Greek religious obligation.

Check with your kaiako or current NZQA guidance before presenting this as a fixed comparison in assessed work.

A World Remembered, Not Witnessed

The Odyssey was composed around the 8th century BCE, but describes a Bronze Age world (c. 1200 BCE) already several centuries gone — including the real, excavated site of Troy, and the Linear B writing system used by Mycenaean palaces long before Homer.

This means the poem functions partly as cultural memory: Iron Age Greeks using an idealised, half-remembered heroic past to comment on their own society's values.

📖

Reception: The Odyssey Rewritten

The poem's afterlife shows how flexible its themes are. Dante places Ulysses in the Inferno for his restless, transgressive curiosity. Tennyson's poem "Ulysses" reimagines an aging king who cannot settle after his nostos. James Joyce's Ulysses transposes the entire structure onto a single day in modern Dublin.

Wider-implications answers can note that later writers keep returning to the tension between Nostos and restless ambition — suggesting Homer's central conflict still resonates well beyond its original context.

🎨

Beyond the Text: Visual & Material Evidence

Some Classical Studies assessments reward evidence beyond the poem itself. Ancient Greek vase painters frequently depicted Odyssey scenes long before any surviving written manuscript — a useful reminder that the story circulated visually as well as orally.

  • • A well-known 5th-century BCE red-figure stamnos (British Museum) shows Odysseus bound to the mast while the Sirens circle the ship — check your library database or the museum's own catalogue for an image if your assessment allows visual sources.
  • • Black-figure vases from the 7th–6th centuries BCE depict the blinding of Polyphemus, often emphasising the collective effort of Odysseus's men — worth comparing to how the poem itself frames that same moment.

This hub doesn't reproduce the artworks themselves — search a museum collection or your library database directly, and check with your teacher whether visual sources are expected for your specific assessment.

AS 91200

Examining Ideas and Values

Achievement Standard 91200 requires you to "examine ideas and values of the classical world." This is a step up from basic reading comprehension; it demands you explore the philosophical and cultural foundations of ancient Greece.

The Thematic Framework

Examinations for this standard consistently focus on four core conceptual areas. Your responses must directly address these themes using specific textual evidence.

  • ✦
    Leadership & Heroism: Expectations of leaders, martial prowess vs. intellect.
  • ✦
    Traditions: Xenia (hospitality), marriage protocols, and the consequences of disruption.
  • ✦
    Religious Beliefs: Fate, divine intervention, and mortal hubris.
  • ✦
    Social Relationships: Power dynamics, duty vs. desire, and the role of the individual.

The Excellence Threshold: "Perception"

To move from Merit (in-depth examination) to Excellence (perceptive examination), you must demonstrate deep insight into the author's didactic purpose.

The Masterclass: Key Themes

To reach Excellence, you must move beyond plot summaries of monster encounters and analyse the underlying societal codes.

The Code of Xenia (Hospitality)

In a Bronze Age world without hotels or police, xenia was a sacred religious duty overseen by Zeus Xenios. It allowed for safe travel and commerce.

  • Ideal: Telemachus welcoming Athena; Eumaeus the swineherd.
  • Violation: The Suitors abusing the oikos; Polyphemus cannibalising guests.
"With a sudden clutch he gripped up two of my men at once and dashed them down upon the ground as though they had been puppies." — Homer, Odyssey Book IX (trans. Samuel Butler, 1900), Polyphemus violating Xenia.

Kleos vs. Nostos

The tension between dying young for glory (Kleos) and surviving to return home (Nostos).

Odysseus's desire for Kleos endangers him, like when he hubristically taunts the Cyclops to ensure his name is known. Conversely, in the Underworld, Achilles rejects Kleos, stating he'd rather be a peasant on earth, elevating Nostos as the ultimate prize.

The Elevation of Metis

Unlike brute strength, the Odyssey celebrates Metis — cunning, intelligence, and strategic patience.

Odysseus relies on his mind to survive Circe and the Sirens. Penelope equals this with her loom trick, deceiving the Suitors to preserve the household. She is the only character cunning enough to outwit Odysseus with the olive-tree bed test.

Preservation of the Oikos

The primary conflict is not monsters, but saving Odysseus's oikos (household/estate) from the parasitic Suitors.

Odysseus's revenge is restoring divine and social order. His rage is centred on the destruction of his household. The disloyal maids are punished for betraying the structural integrity of the oikos.

Level 2 Essential Lexicon

Using specific Greek terminology accurately elevates your writing and demonstrates informed knowledge.

Xenia

The reciprocal code of guest-host friendship, considered a sacred religious duty protected by Zeus Xenios.

Oikos

The family, household, and estate; the fundamental economic and social unit of ancient Greek society.

Metis

Cunning intelligence, adaptability, and strategic patience. The defining trait of Odysseus and Penelope.

Kleos

Immortal glory, typically won on the battlefield. Often contrasted with Nostos in the Odyssey.

Nostos

The journey home, or homecoming. The driving force of the epic.

Hubris

Excessive pride or arrogance, usually leading to mortal downfall or divine punishment.

Strategic Directives

  • 1. Attack the Prompt: Do not write a generic intro summarising the plot. Your first sentence must address the keywords of the question.

The Solution:

The Pedagogical Framework of Standards 91394 & 91200

Both standards require candidates to analyse or examine "ideas and values of the classical world." To excel, candidates must transition from plot summary to a highly analytical, ideologically-driven framework.

The pinnacle of these standards, "Achievement with Excellence," requires "perceptive" analysis. This means explicitly deconstructing why Homer constructed a scene the way he did. Insight is consistently demonstrated when candidates weave primary evidence seamlessly into argument, show how classical values conflict or are tested, and connect character actions to Homer's didactic purpose for his original audience.

Exemplar 1: Leadership & Power

Odysseus: Effective yet Flawed

X-Ray:
Plain English: Odysseus is a smart, clever leader when he tricks the Cyclops, but he's also reckless — bragging about his real name almost gets his whole crew killed. Later, he can't stop his men from eating the forbidden cattle, and they all die for it. So he's a great leader in some ways and a failed one in others.
Homer's characterisation of Odysseus deliberately complicates the heroic ideal of leadership, presenting a figure whose arete is undercut by recurring lapses in judgement. In the Cyclops episode, Odysseus's metis allows him to out-think Polyphemus, escaping strapped beneath the rams' bellies — Homer introduces him as "renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety, so that my fame ascends to heaven" (Book IX), a stratagem entirely in keeping with that reputation. Yet this same brilliance curdles into hubris the moment he shouts his true name back at the blinded Cyclops, a decision that invites Poseidon's decade-long wrath and directly delays his nostos. This same failure of restraint resurfaces on Thrinacia, where Odysseus's inability to control his starving crew results in the slaughter of the Cattle of Helios and the death of every remaining man. Homer's didactic point is therefore not that Odysseus is simply heroic, but that leadership demanding both cunning and self-mastery is precarious — even the greatest hero remains subject to hubris.

Exemplar 2: Tradition & Social Order

Xenia Violated and Restored

X-Ray:
Plain English: The Suitors break the sacred rule of guest-friendship by eating Odysseus's food and disrespecting his family for years. When Odysseus finally comes home in disguise, he tests people's hospitality, then kills the Suitors in a bloody scene that the poem frames as justice, not just revenge.
The disruption and restoration of xenia structures the entire second half of the epic, functioning as Homer's central measure of social and moral order. Because the Suitors occupy Odysseus's oikos for over three years, consuming his herds and pressuring Penelope, their behaviour constitutes a sustained violation of the guest-host code — they take without reciprocity, the defining betrayal of xenia. This violation is deliberately tested and confirmed in Book XVII, when the disguised Odysseus, posing as a beggar, is struck for asking for food: Antinous "threw a footstool at him, and hit him on the right shoulder-blade near the top of his back." Consequently, the mnesterophonia in Book XXII is framed not as vengeance but as the restoration of dike — Homer signals this by having "Jove's daughter Minerva" come "up to them, having assumed the voice and form of Mentor" to fight alongside Odysseus, aligning the violence with divine sanction. The episode thus reinforces to its aristocratic audience that hospitality was not mere etiquette but a cosmically enforced obligation.

Exemplar 3: Social Relationships

Metis as a Shared, Not Gendered, Trait

X-Ray:
Plain English: Odysseus survives using his brain, not just muscle — outsmarting monsters and enemies with tricks. Penelope does the same thing back home, using her own cleverness (the weaving trick, testing Odysseus with the bed) to protect the household while the men are away or acting rashly.
Homer consistently privileges metis over bia, constructing intelligence rather than brute strength as the epic's defining heroic currency. This is most visible when Odysseus escapes Polyphemus not through force but through a pun on his own name, telling the Cyclops "my name is Noman; this is what my father and mother and my friends have always called me" (Greek Outis), so that when Polyphemus later cries "Noman is killing me by fraud! Noman is killing me by force!" his kinsmen assume nothing is wrong and leave him unaided. Crucially, this trait is not gendered male: Penelope demonstrates equivalent metis through the three-year loom trick, unweaving Laertes's shroud by night to stall the Suitors, and later by testing Odysseus with the immovable marriage bed — a trap that verifies his identity through shared secret knowledge rather than appearance. Because husband and wife are shown to be intellectual equals, Homer positions their reunion as a meeting of matched cunning, not merely a romantic resolution — a notably progressive framing of the oikos's female head.

Exemplar 4: Wider Implications

The Odyssey as Didactic Blueprint

X-Ray:
Plain English: The poem isn't just an adventure story — it's teaching its original audience how a good household and a fair society should work. It rewards loyalty (like the swineherd Eumaeus), punishes disloyalty (the unfaithful maids), and shows that even kings answer to the gods.
Beyond its narrative surface, the Odyssey functions as a didactic blueprint for aristocratic Greek society, using the fate of individual characters to model and enforce correct social behaviour. This is evident in the contrast Homer draws between loyal and disloyal members of the oikos: Eumaeus the swineherd, who maintains the household in his master's absence, is rewarded with continued place and dignity, whereas the disloyal maidservants who consorted with the Suitors are punished by execution, a severity that signals the household's structural integrity was treated as sacrosanct. Divine oversight reinforces this lesson: because Athena repeatedly intervenes to guide and protect Odysseus, Homer implies that legitimate power is contingent on divine favour, not birth alone — meaning even a king's arete requires validation from beyond the mortal social order.

Strategic Essay Execution

1. Decoding the Question

The most common error is failing to directly answer the prompt. If asked about leadership, an essay on the Cyclops episode fails unless it explicitly analyses what that episode reveals about leadership.

Commit to a focus: If given a choice of characters or values, choose the strongest evidence base and stay with it.

2. Weaving in Primary Evidence

Evidence must serve the argument, not decorate it. Use the Concept → Evidence → Analysis pattern.

Don't just cite: "Odysseus blinds the Cyclops" alone is description. Add: "...an act of hubris that invites a decade of divine punishment, revealing that even arete has limits."

Confusable Pairs Drill

Excellence-level precision often comes down to not mixing up near-synonyms. These four pairs are the ones students most often blur together.

Kleos

Glory won through action — what others say about you afterwards, often after death. A legacy.

Timé

Honour and status owed to you right now, through respect, gifts, and rank — a live social currency.

Quick test: Dying gloriously and being remembered forever = kleos. Expecting the best seat at a feast because of your rank = timé.

Dike

The abstract principle of justice and moral order — what should happen.

Nemesis

The actual downfall or retribution that punishes hubris — what does happen to restore dike.

Quick test: Dike is the law; nemesis is the enforcement of it.

Metis

Cunning, strategic intelligence, wit, and resourcefulness.

Bia

Raw physical force and violent strength.

Quick test: Escaping the Cyclops with the "Noman" trick = metis. Smashing through a door = bia.

Xenia

Hospitality specifically between a host and a stranger or guest — ritualised and protected by Zeus.

Philia

Affectionate bond or friendship between equals — no hospitality ritual required.

Quick test: Feeding a shipwrecked stranger = xenia. The bond between old friends or family = philia.

Full Model Essay

The exemplars above show strong individual paragraphs. This shows how a complete response is paced from introduction to conclusion.

Homer's characterisation of Odysseus offers a complex model of classical leadership — one that is, to a significant extent, both exemplary and deeply flawed. Rather than presenting an uncomplicated warrior-king, the poem constructs a figure whose arete depends as much on metis as on physical courage, while simultaneously warning that the same qualities which make him an effective leader in isolated crises can undermine his ability to protect his men collectively. This essay argues that Odysseus's leadership is conditionally effective: successful when cunning and self-restraint work together, but precarious whenever ambition for kleos overrides responsibility to his community.

The clearest evidence of Odysseus's leadership strength lies in the Cyclops episode, where his metis directly saves his surviving crew from Polyphemus. Introduced as a man "renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety," Odysseus devises the "Noman" trick, allowing his men to escape strapped beneath the Cyclops's rams undetected. This is leadership defined by intelligence rather than brute force — a values system classical Greek society clearly privileged, given how consistently the poem rewards cunning over bia. To this extent, Odysseus embodies the ideal that a good leader thinks his way out of danger rather than merely fighting his way out.

However, this same self-assurance curdles into hubris in the very same episode, complicating any straightforwardly positive reading of his leadership. Odysseus's decision to shout his true name back to the blinded Cyclops — driven by a desire for kleos rather than any practical necessity — directly causes Poseidon's decade-long persecution of his voyage home. Here, leadership tips from calculated risk-taking into reckless self-interest: Odysseus prioritises his own legacy over his men's safety, delaying the entire crew's nostos for the sake of being remembered. This suggests that, to a significant degree, classical society viewed unchecked pride as a genuine threat to good leadership, regardless of how capable the leader might otherwise be.

This failure recurs, more catastrophically, on Thrinacia. Despite Tiresias's explicit warning not to touch the Cattle of the Sun, Odysseus's authority proves insufficient to control his starving, mutinous crew, and the resulting slaughter of Helios's cattle brings a divine punishment that kills every remaining man except Odysseus himself. Unlike the Cyclops episode, where cunning at least partially redeems his hubris, here Odysseus's leadership fails outright — he cannot translate personal survival instinct into collective discipline. Read alongside the Cyclops episode, this suggests Homer is deliberately building a pattern: Odysseus's leadership is only as strong as his self-restraint in the moment, and that restraint fails precisely when his own glory is at stake.

Ultimately, then, Odysseus's leadership reflects classical values only conditionally. He models the intelligence-over-strength ideal that Homer's audience is clearly meant to admire, but he equally models the danger of hubris undermining even the most capable leader. Rather than offering audiences a simple heroic template, Homer constructs Odysseus's journey as a didactic warning: greatness and fallibility are not opposites in the classical worldview, but two sides of the same heroic coin — and true leadership requires not just cunning, but the humility to restrain it.
